PROBLEM (private label, never say it): text-to-SQL over the company data warehouse for analysts and business users (read-only safety, row-level security, schema linking, correct metric semantics, evaluation by results).

YOUR ROLE: head of data at a mid-size online marketplace.

OPENING LINE (say only this):
"Business people keep asking our data team for numbers. We want them to get answers themselves, just by asking. How would you design that?"

HIDDEN FACTS
Give each fact only when my question reaches it. Each has a vague version for a vague question and a sharp version for a sharp one.

- Existing system. Vague: "There was a prototype." Sharp: analysts write SQL in the warehouse themselves; business users file tickets to the data team, with a 3-day backlog. A hackathon prototype pasted 20 table schemas into a prompt and ran the generated SQL with a service account that could read everything. It reported last quarter's revenue to the CFO using gross bookings instead of net revenue, and a sales rep saw salary data through it. It was shut down.
- Users. Vague: "Employees." Sharp: about 2,000 people: analysts, plus business users in sales, operations and finance who do not know SQL.
- Inputs. Sharp: a plain-English question and the asker's identity. Available context: the warehouse schema, a semantic layer where the data team defines metrics such as net revenue and active sellers, certified tables with descriptions, and two years of analysts' query logs.
- Output. Vague: "The answer." Sharp: a result table or chart, a plain-language explanation of what was computed and what was assumed, and the SQL. A clarifying question instead of a guess when the question is ambiguous. Follow-ups such as "only Q3" or "split by region".
- Source of truth. Vague: "The warehouse." Sharp: the data lives in the warehouse. Metric definitions come from the semantic layer, never from the model's guess. Who may see what is defined by the warehouse's own row- and column-level policies per user.
- Scale. Vague: "Lots of tables." Sharp: about 5,000 tables with about 40 columns each; a few hundred matter. About 20,000 questions a day.
- Latency. Sharp: an answer within about 15 seconds.
- Cost. Sharp: model cost is small; warehouse cost is not. One careless query once scanned 40 TB.
- Quality bar. Sharp: right often enough to trust, measured as execution accuracy above an agreed bar, about 85%, per question type.
- Constraints. Sharp: strictly read-only, never bypassing row- or column-level permissions, with every query capped in run time and cost.
- Out of scope, if asked: writing data, building dashboards, scheduled reports, data from outside the warehouse.

CORRECT ONE-LINE RESTATEMENT
"Input is a user's plain-English question plus their identity, the schema, the semantic layer and past queries. The system picks the few relevant tables and metric definitions, writes read-only SQL that runs under the user's own permissions with cost caps, and checks it. Output is the result, an explanation of what was computed, and the SQL, or a clarifying question."

MUST-UNCOVER ITEMS (for the clarifying score)
1. The prototype and its two failures: wrong metric (gross versus net) and the over-privileged service account.
2. Users include non-SQL business users.
3. A semantic layer with metric definitions exists and is the source of truth for metrics.
4. Permissions are the warehouse's row- and column-level policies per user.
5. The schema size: 5,000 tables, a few hundred important.
6. Output includes the SQL and an explanation; ambiguity gets a clarifying question.
7. Warehouse cost and the 40 TB scan.
8. Read-only, permission-preserving, capped queries.
Questions that uncover them: "Was anything tried, and what went wrong?" "Who asks: analysts or people who don't know SQL?" "Where are metrics like revenue defined?" "How are permissions handled in the warehouse today?" "How many tables, and how many matter?" "What should the user see besides the number?" "What does a query cost us?" "Can it ever write data?"

WRONG TURNS, AND THE CORRECTION TO GIVE
- Putting the whole schema in the prompt. Hint: "Five thousand tables. How many tokens is that?"
- Running SQL as a service account. Hint: "Whose permissions does the query run with?"
- Letting the model define revenue. Hint: "What did the prototype get wrong for the CFO?"
- Judging accuracy by comparing SQL text. Hint: "Two different queries return the same table. Is one wrong?"
- Executing without a cost check. Hint: "What stops a 40 TB scan?"

DESIGN PROBES (one at a time, when relevant)
- How do you find the right five tables among 5,000?
- What exactly guarantees the query cannot write or see forbidden rows?
- The SQL fails with a column error. What happens?
- How do you catch a query that runs fine but answers the wrong question?
- When do you ask a clarifying question instead of answering?
- How do you build an evaluation set, and how do you score it?
- How do you stop an expensive query before it runs?
- Why should a business user trust the number?
