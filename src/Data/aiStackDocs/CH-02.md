An employee asks the new HR assistant a simple question: "How many weeks of parental leave do I get?" The answer is sitting in the Employee Handbook, section 4.2, in a table: 26 weeks. The plain model, asked the same thing, said "around 12 weeks". Chapter 1 ended with the problem: the model never saw the handbook, and you cannot paste all 3,000 internal documents into every prompt.

Think about how you would answer it yourself. You would not memorise all 3,000 documents. You would open the right policy, find the right paragraph, read it, and answer. That is an **open-book exam**, and it is exactly the idea behind this chapter.

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** splits answering into two jobs:

- **Retrieval**: find the few passages that answer the question.
- **Generation**: hand those passages to the model with the question, and tell it to answer only from them and to say which passage it used.

The model stops being the source of facts and becomes a reader of evidence you give it. In this chapter you will build Ask HR, the assistant for a company of 5,000 employees, one attempt at a time. Each attempt fixes the last one's problem and reveals the next.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    V0["Attempt 0:<br/>paste the whole<br/>policy"] --> V1["Attempt 1:<br/>keyword search"]
    V1 --> V2["Attempt 2:<br/>search by meaning<br/>with embeddings"]
    V2 --> V3["Attempt 3:<br/>cut documents<br/>into chunks"]
    V3 --> V4["Ask HR v1:<br/>the full RAG<br/>pipeline"]
```

## 1. Attempt 0: just paste the document

For one question about one document, the simplest thing works. Copy the 40-page Employee Handbook into the prompt, add the question, and ask the model to answer from it. It answers correctly and can even quote the page.

This is worth remembering, because it is the right design in some real cases: when the knowledge is small (a few dozen pages) and rarely changes, put all of it in the prompt every time and turn on prompt caching so the repeated part is cheap. No search means no search mistakes.

But employees ask about everything: leave, expenses, travel, payroll, laptops, VPN problems. All of it is 3,000 documents, around twenty million tokens. It cannot fit, and even the part that fits would be slow, expensive and less accurate (chapter 1, section 5).

> **Pause and think.** For each question, only two or three pages matter. What is the most obvious way to find them?

## 2. Attempt 1: search by keywords

The obvious way is the way you search your own files: look for documents containing the words of the question. This is **keyword search**, and it is decades old and very good at what it does.

It handles "What does error E-4012 mean on the payroll portal?" perfectly: exactly one IT runbook contains that string.

Then an employee types: "How much time off do I get when my baby is born?" The handbook says: "Parental leave: employees are entitled to 26 weeks of paid leave." Look at the two sentences. They share almost no words. "Time off" versus "leave", "baby is born" versus "parental". Keyword search ranks the time-off request form first (it contains "time" and "off") and the parental leave policy ninth.

People describe the same thing in endlessly different words. A search that only matches spelling will keep missing.

> **Pause and think.** To a human, "time off when my baby is born" and "parental leave" obviously mean nearly the same thing. How could a computer measure that two sentences mean the same thing, when they share no words?

## 3. Attempt 2: search by meaning with embeddings

### Meaning as a location on a map

Imagine a map where every sentence has a location, and sentences that mean similar things are placed close together. "Parental leave: 26 weeks" and "time off when my baby is born" are neighbours. "Reset your VPN" lives in a different neighbourhood. If you had such a map, searching by meaning would be easy: put the question on the map and look at what is nearby.

An **embedding** is exactly that location. An **embedding model** reads a piece of text and outputs a fixed-length list of numbers, a **vector**, typically 384 to 3,072 numbers long. Each vector is a point in a space with that many directions. Nobody can picture 768 directions, but the idea is the same as a 2D map, so here is one squeezed down to two:

```mermaid
quadrantChart
    title Meaning space, squeezed into 2 dimensions
    x-axis About systems --> About people
    y-axis About things --> About time
    quadrant-1 people and time
    quadrant-2 systems and time
    quadrant-3 systems and things
    quadrant-4 people and money
    parental leave 26 weeks: [0.84, 0.8]
    time off when baby is born: [0.78, 0.7]
    leave starts 4 weeks early: [0.7, 0.78]
    password expires every 90 days: [0.2, 0.76]
    reset your VPN: [0.15, 0.3]
    new laptop request: [0.28, 0.2]
    travel reimbursement: [0.75, 0.25]
```

- The question "time off when my baby is born" lands right next to "parental leave 26 weeks", even though they share no words.
- "Reset your VPN" lands among the IT texts, far away.
- In the real 768-dimensional space, the axes do not have human names like "about money"; the model learned its own directions. The picture is a simplification of the idea, not of the actual numbers.

**Vector search** is then: embed the question, find the stored vectors nearest to it, return their texts. A **vector index** is the data structure that answers "which stored vectors are nearest to this one?" quickly (chapter 3 opens it up).

### Measuring "near"

Three common ways to measure how close two vectors are:

- **Cosine similarity** compares the directions the two vectors point in, ignoring their length. 1 means same direction, 0 means unrelated.
- **Dot product**: multiply the two vectors number by number and add it all up.
- **Euclidean (L2) distance**: the straight-line distance between the two points.

A practical trick: scale every vector to length 1 when you store it (**normalise** it). Then cosine similarity and dot product give the same answer, and Euclidean distance ranks results in the same order. So you normalise once and use the dot product, the cheapest to compute.

### How does the model learn where to put things?

This is the part interviewers love, because it explains *why* embeddings work. An embedding model is trained on millions of pairs of texts that belong together: a question and its answer, a search query and the page people clicked, a title and its article. Training uses **contrastive learning**: pull matching pairs together, push non-matching ones apart.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    subgraph BATCH["One training batch"]
        Q1["Q: weeks of<br/>parental leave?"] --- D1["A: 26 weeks,<br/>fully paid"]
        Q2["Q: reset my<br/>VPN?"] --- D2["A: open the IT<br/>portal, then..."]
        Q3["Q: per diem<br/>in London?"] --- D3["A: 60 GBP<br/>a day"]
    end
    BATCH --> L["Score: each question<br/>should be nearest to<br/>its own answer"]
    L --> U["Nudge the model's<br/>numbers so pairs<br/>move closer"]
    U --> BATCH
```

- Each question in the batch has its correct answer. All the *other* answers in the batch serve as wrong answers for free (**in-batch negatives**).
- The model computes a **loss**, a score of how wrong it currently is: high if a question sits closer to a wrong answer than to its own, low if every question is nearest its own answer.
- Training nudges the model's billions of numbers, a tiny bit each step, to make the loss smaller. After millions of steps, texts that answer each other end up close together.
- The best training sets include **hard negatives**: wrong answers that look very similar to the right one, such as the leave carry-over rule for a parental leave question. They teach fine distinctions.

### Choosing an embedding model

- **Test on your own questions.** Run three or four candidate models on 50-100 real questions where you know the right document, and keep the one that finds it most often. Public leaderboards such as MTEB are a good shortlist, not a decision: their tasks are not your documents.
- **Check the input limit.** Each model reads at most a fixed number of tokens (often 512) and silently ignores the rest.
- **Dimensions cost memory and speed.** More numbers per vector can hold finer meaning but take more storage and slower comparisons. Some models are trained so that the first part of the vector works on its own (**Matryoshka embeddings**, after the nesting dolls): keep 256 of 1,024 numbers and it still works, a little less precisely.
- **Use the prefixes.** Many models expect questions and documents to be marked differently, for example `query: ...` and `passage: ...`. Forgetting this quietly lowers quality.
- **Languages and vocabulary.** If employees mix Hindi and English, test exactly that mix. If your company's own words ("WFA", "comp-off", internal project names) keep confusing the model, you can **fine-tune the embedding model** on your own question-and-document pairs; section 9 shows how.

Correction: switching embedding models later is not a configuration change. Vectors from different models live in different spaces and cannot be compared, so every document must be embedded again.

> **Pause and think.** You embed the whole 40-page Employee Handbook as one vector and ask "how many weeks of parental leave do I get?". The handbook covers leave, expenses, travel, IT, conduct and benefits. What does a single point on the map for all of that look like, and will it be close to this specific question?

## 4. Attempt 3: cut documents into chunks

One vector for 40 pages is an average of everything in it, a blurry point somewhere in the middle of "HR-ish" space. It matches every HR question weakly and none of them sharply. And when it is retrieved, it drags 20,000 tokens into the prompt.

So documents are cut into **chunks**: pieces of a few hundred tokens, each embedded and searched on its own. Think of cutting a book into index cards, one idea per card.

Chunk size is a trade-off between two failures:

- **Too big**: the vector averages many topics and matches nothing sharply; every hit is expensive to paste.
- **Too small**: "It is 26 weeks for the primary carer." What is? A tiny chunk loses the context that gives it meaning.

### Ways to cut

- **Fixed-size with overlap**: every 400 tokens, with the last 50 tokens repeated at the start of the next chunk, so a sentence cut at the border appears whole in one of them. Simple, but it slices through sentences and tables.
- **Recursive**: try to split on paragraph breaks; if a piece is still too big, split it on line breaks, then on sentences, until every piece fits. A strong default (LangChain, a popular library covered in chapter 5, ships one called `RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter`).
- **Structure-aware**: split on the document's own structure, headings, sections, list items and table rows, keeping each heading attached to its text.
- **Semantic**: embed each sentence and start a new chunk wherever neighbouring sentences stop being similar in meaning.

Real document collections need a mix:

```mermaid
flowchart TB
    D{"What kind of<br/>document?"} -->|"policies with<br/>headings"| H["Split on headings,<br/>keep the heading path<br/>with every chunk"]
    D -->|"runbooks,<br/>step lists"| L["Keep each procedure<br/>whole if it fits"]
    D -->|"tables"| T["One chunk per row,<br/>column names<br/>repeated"]
    D -->|"long prose"| R["Recursive,<br/>300-500 tokens"]
    D -->|"FAQ pages"| F["One question +<br/>answer per chunk"]
    H --> M["Add metadata:<br/>document, version,<br/>section, who may<br/>see it, date"]
    L --> M
    T --> M
    R --> M
    F --> M
```

- **Headings are context.** A chunk that starts "Leave can start 4 weeks before the due date..." is far easier to find when it carries "Employee Handbook > Leave > Parental leave".
- **Tables need their column names.** "UK | 26 | full pay" means nothing alone; "Parental leave. Country: UK. Weeks: 26. Pay: full salary." is findable and readable.
- **Metadata** is extra information stored with each chunk: which document and version it came from, the section, which groups may see it, the date. You will filter and cite with it constantly.

Two rules people get wrong: count **tokens**, not characters (the embedding model silently cuts off anything past its limit), and keep overlap modest (0-20%), because more overlap just duplicates content.

**Decision point: what chunk size should Ask HR use?** Do not guess; measure. Take 80 real employee questions, mark the passage that answers each, build the index three times at 200, 400 and 800 tokens per chunk, and count how often the right passage lands in the top 10 results for each. Keep the winner. "512 because everyone uses 512" is the weak interview answer. "I compared three sizes on our real questions" is the strong one.

### Two upgrades that fix the "too small" problem

**Parent-child retrieval** gets precision and context at once. Embed small chunks (a few sentences) because small chunks have sharp meaning; each remembers the bigger section it came from, its **parent**. Search the small ones, send the parents to the model.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    subgraph DOC["Employee Handbook 2026"]
        P3["Section 4.2:<br/>Parental leave<br/>1,200 tokens"]
        P3 --> c12["child: 26 weeks<br/>for the primary carer"]
        P3 --> c13["child: tell your<br/>manager 8 weeks ahead"]
    end
    Q["How long is<br/>parental leave?"] --> S["Search the<br/>small children"]
    c12 --> S
    S --> L["Send the whole<br/>parent section<br/>to the model"]
```

The model now also sees the notice rule sitting next to the 26 weeks.

**Contextual retrieval** (published by Anthropic) fixes chunks that make no sense alone. Before indexing, a model reads each chunk together with its whole document and writes one sentence that places it: "From the 2026 Employee Handbook, section 4.2, parental leave." That sentence is glued to the front of the chunk before embedding. Anthropic reported this cut failed retrievals by 35%, and by 49% when the keyword index got the same treatment. The cost is one model call per chunk at indexing time.

## 5. Ask HR v1: the full pipeline

Put the pieces together and you have the standard RAG architecture. It is two pipelines that run at different times:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    subgraph IDX["Offline: whenever documents change"]
        D1["3,000 docs"] --> P1["Parse:<br/>text, tables,<br/>headings"]
        P1 --> C1["Chunk +<br/>metadata"]
        C1 --> E1["Embed each<br/>chunk"]
        E1 --> V[("Vector index")]
        C1 --> K[("Keyword index")]
    end
    subgraph ASK["Online: every question"]
        Q["Employee's question"] --> R["Embed it, search,<br/>keep the best 20"]
        R --> RR["Rerank,<br/>keep the best 5"]
        RR --> PR["Prompt: rules +<br/>5 chunks with ids +<br/>question"]
        PR --> A["Answer that cites<br/>[hb-2026:4.2]"]
    end
    V --> R
    K --> R
```

**Offline**, whenever a document is created, edited or deleted: parse it into clean text, cut it into chunks with metadata, embed each chunk, and store it in the vector index. The same chunks also go into a keyword index, because, as you saw in attempt 1, keyword search still wins on exact codes. (Chapter 3 shows how to combine the two properly.)

**Online**, for every question:

1. Embed the question with the same embedding model.
2. Search for the best 20 candidate chunks, keeping only chunks this employee is allowed to see.
3. A **reranker**, a slower and more accurate model, rereads those 20 and keeps the best 5 (chapter 3).
4. Build the prompt: instructions, the 5 chunks each labelled with an id like `[hb-2026:4.2]`, then the question.
5. The model answers and cites the ids, so employees can click through and check.

### Building the prompt

Order matters as much as content:

- **Instructions first**: who the assistant is and the rules. "Answer only from the documents below. If they do not answer the question, say you could not find it. Cite the id of every passage you use."
- **The documents next**, each wrapped with its id and a little metadata: `[hb-2026:4.2 | Employee Handbook 2026 | updated 2026-03-01]`. The date lets the model prefer the newer of two conflicting passages.
- **The question last.**

Fixed parts first means prompt caching can reuse them across questions. Remove near-duplicate chunks before building the prompt (the handbook and an FAQ quoting it), so one fact does not take up five slots.

A token budget for one call might be: instructions 800, five chunks of 400 = 2,000, recent conversation 500, question 100, room for the answer 500. About 3,900 tokens. When answers need more evidence, the fix is better chunks or better ranking, not more chunks: more text often makes answers worse (chapter 4).

### Citations you can trust

Ask the model for structured output with two fields, `answer` and `citations`. Afterwards, in code, drop any cited id that was not actually retrieved, and run a check: for each sentence, a second model call answers "is this sentence supported by the chunk it cites?". Unsupported sentences are removed, or the assistant says it could not find the answer.

### Where the time goes

For one answer: embedding the question 10-50 ms, both searches 10-50 ms, reranking 50-300 ms, and generation, which dominates everything. **Stream** the answer, showing words as they are produced, so people start reading after the first word instead of waiting for the last. Also cache: embeddings of repeated questions, search results for very frequent questions (cleared whenever the index changes), and the fixed start of the prompt.

## 6. Keeping the index honest when documents change

The handbook is updated: the 2026 version raises parental leave from 16 weeks to 26. What has to happen?

Every chunk is stored with its `doc_id` and `version`. On an update: re-parse and re-chunk the document, embed and store the new chunks, and delete all old chunks of that `doc_id`, in the vector index and the keyword index, as one operation. Change events come from the wiki's webhooks, or a nightly job that compares a fingerprint (hash) of every document's content.

Correction: teams often add the new chunks and forget the delete. Then the 2025 and 2026 versions are both searchable and answers mix them: "16 weeks" and "26 weeks" in the same week. Worse, a document that was deleted, or that some people lost access to, stays searchable. That is a data leak, not just a stale answer.

Bigger changes, such as a new chunk size or a new embedding model, mean rebuilding the whole index. Doing that in place would leave a half-old, half-new index for hours. The safe pattern is **blue-green re-indexing**: build the new index next to the old one, keep both updated, test the new one on your 80 questions, switch queries over in one step, and delete the old one after a safety period.

## 7. The numbers behind the index

A quick estimate makes any design answer stronger:

- 3,000 documents, around 20 million tokens: at 400 tokens per chunk, about 50,000 chunks.
- One embedding: 1,024 numbers x 4 bytes = about 4 KB. 50,000 chunks = about 0.2 GB of vectors, plus the index's own overhead. It fits in memory on one machine.
- Embedding everything once: 20 million tokens. At an assumed $0.02 per million tokens for an embedding API, about $0.40. Embedding is cheap; generating answers is where the money goes.

## 8. When RAG is the wrong tool

Interviewers rarely ask "what is RAG?". They ask "why RAG, and not something else?".

```mermaid
flowchart TB
    Q{"What is missing<br/>from the answer?"} -->|"facts the model<br/>never saw"| K{"Small and fixed,<br/>or large and<br/>changing?"}
    Q -->|"a behaviour:<br/>format, tone,<br/>narrow task"| B["Prompting first;<br/>fine-tuning if that<br/>is not enough"]
    Q -->|"live data:<br/>leave balance,<br/>ticket status"| T["Ask the live<br/>system (a tool,<br/>chapter 5)"]
    Q -->|"counts and totals"| SQL["A database query,<br/>not search"]
    K -->|"small, fixed"| S["Put it all in the<br/>prompt, cache it"]
    K -->|"large, changing,<br/>needs citations or<br/>per-user access"| R["RAG"]
```

"How many leave requests were filed last month?" is the trap question: search returns *some* leave documents; it cannot count all leave records. That is a database query.

## 9. When the embedding model does not speak your company

After a month, one weakness remains: employees write "WFA" for work-from-anywhere days and "comp-off" for a day off in lieu, and the general embedding model puts "WFA days left" near articles about workflow automation. Fixes, in order of cost:

1. Keyword search alongside vector search (chapter 3), which catches exact terms.
2. Contextual sentences and good metadata on chunks.
3. Trying other embedding models on your 80 questions.
4. **Fine-tuning the embedding model** on your own pairs:

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    S["Real pairs: employee<br/>questions + the chunk<br/>they clicked"] --> P["10k-50k<br/>question-chunk pairs"]
    G["Generated pairs:<br/>a model writes<br/>questions per chunk"] --> P
    P --> HN["Add hard negatives:<br/>similar but wrong<br/>chunks"]
    HN --> T["Fine-tune a small<br/>open embedding<br/>model"]
    T --> E["Compare on the<br/>80 test questions"]
    E -->|"better"| R["Re-embed everything,<br/>switch blue-green"]
```

A few thousand good pairs can noticeably improve search on domain vocabulary. The cost is owning a model: hosting it, and re-embedding everything when it changes. So it comes last.

Correction: "the top model on the leaderboard will be best for us" is often false. A smaller model tuned on your own pairs can beat it on your data.

## 10. Complete example: the smallest real RAG

```python
import numpy as np

def build_index(chunks, embed):
    vecs = np.array(embed([c["text"] for c in chunks]), dtype=np.float32)
    vecs /= np.linalg.norm(vecs, axis=1, keepdims=True)      # normalise once
    return vecs

def search(question, chunks, vecs, embed, k=5, allowed=None):
    q = np.array(embed([question])[0], dtype=np.float32)
    q /= np.linalg.norm(q)
    scores = vecs @ q                                        # dot == cosine now
    order = np.argsort(-scores)
    hits = [chunks[i] for i in order if allowed is None or chunks[i]["group"] in allowed]
    return hits[:k]

def build_prompt(question, hits):
    docs = "\n\n".join(f"[{h['id']} | {h['title']} | {h['updated']}]\n{h['text']}" for h in hits)
    return (
        "You answer employees' HR questions using ONLY the documents below.\n"
        "If they do not contain the answer, say you could not find it.\n"
        "Cite the id of every document you use, like [hb-2026:4.2].\n\n"
        f"<documents>\n{docs}\n</documents>\n\nQuestion: {question}"
    )
```

Expected behaviour:

- "time off when my baby is born?" retrieves the parental-leave chunk first, although they share almost no words, because their vectors are close.
- An employee outside a group that may see a document (say, the managers-only salary bands) never gets its chunks, because they are filtered out before the prompt is built.
- If no chunk is relevant, the instructions tell the model to say it could not find the answer, rather than invent one.

Notice a quiet problem in `search`: it scores every stored vector on every question, and it filters *after* ranking. Remember both; they are the start of the next chapter.

## What breaks next

Ask HR v1 goes live and employees love it. So the company builds the same design into its product: a support assistant for its 500 customer companies, each searching only its own workspace's documents, runbooks and past tickets. That is 10 million chunks. Within two weeks, three complaints arrive:

1. **It is slow.** Every question compares against all 10 million chunk vectors: 10 million x 1,024 multiplications, about 10 billion operations per question, and a search takes 2.4 seconds.
2. **Workspace "acme" gets no results** for its own runbooks, although they are definitely indexed. Acme owns 1% of the chunks.
3. **Error codes fail again.** "Login fails with ERR_TOKEN_EXPIRED" returns vague chunks about login trouble in general. Meaning search does not know what that code means, and the exact-match strength of keyword search was lost.

> **Pause and think.** How could you find the nearest vectors without comparing against all 10 million? Why might a filter for "only workspace acme" make results disappear rather than just shrink? And how would you combine a keyword ranking and a meaning ranking whose scores are on completely different scales?

That is chapter 3.
